Home maintenance records: what to keep and how to organize them

A useful home record helps you answer three questions: what do I own, what happened to it, and what needs attention next?

Do not save everything in one pile.

A smaller organized record is more useful than a box of papers you cannot search. Keep the documents that explain ownership, condition, cost, service, coverage, and future obligations.

Which home maintenance records should you keep?

Keep the documents that show what you own, the work completed, what you paid, any coverage, and the next action. Start with these five folders; the sections below explain what belongs in each.

  1. Property: closing documents, inspection findings, permits, plans, and relevant approvals.
  2. Systems: equipment labels, model and serial numbers, manuals, installation information, and service requirements.
  3. Warranties: receipts, written coverage, registration confirmations, claim instructions, and correspondence.
  4. Maintenance and repairs: dated log entries linked to invoices, payment evidence, photos, and follow-up tasks.
  5. Contractors: business contacts, written scopes, estimates, agreements, and change orders.

You do not need to reconstruct every missing document before starting. Record what you can verify now, mark unknown dates as unknown, and add older records as you find them.

Property and purchase records

  • Purchase agreement, settlement or closing documents, deed information, and title records
  • Home inspection reports and documentation of repairs completed before closing
  • Surveys, permits, certificates, plans, and approved drawings that apply to the property
  • Property-tax records and relevant association or condominium documents

Store original legal documents according to professional advice and keep a secure working copy where appropriate.

Home systems and equipment records

Create one record for each major system or appliance. Include:

  • Equipment name and location
  • Manufacturer, model, and serial number
  • Installation or approximate age
  • Filter, fuel, battery, or routine supply information
  • Last service date and service provider
  • Warranty expiration and registration details
  • Manual, receipt, and clear photos of labels

Common records include heating and cooling equipment, water heater, electrical service, pumps, generators, water treatment, septic or well equipment, major appliances, alarms, and specialized systems.

Repairs, maintenance, and improvements

For meaningful work, keep the date, location, problem, written scope, contractor, cost, permit information, warranty, invoices, proof of payment, and before-and-after photos. Record routine service even when no repair was needed.

The IRS advises homeowners to keep clear records supporting the basis and adjusted basis of a home, including purchase records and evidence of qualifying improvements. Tax treatment depends on the work and the owner’s situation, so use current IRS guidance or a tax professional rather than labeling every repair as an improvement.

Warranties and service contracts

  • Coverage document and exclusions
  • Purchase and installation date
  • Registration confirmation
  • Claim instructions and required maintenance
  • Claim numbers, communications, and repair outcomes

The FTC recommends keeping the product receipt with the written warranty, so purchase details and coverage can be found together.

Do not assume a builder warranty, manufacturer warranty, and home service contract provide the same coverage. Read each document and track deadlines.

Contractor and professional records

Keep business name, contact details, license or registration information where applicable, proof of insurance provided for the project, written estimates, contracts, change orders, permits, invoices, warranties, and notes about the completed work.

Photos and home inventory

Take dated photos of equipment labels, completed projects, recurring problem areas, valuable belongings, and each room. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners notes that an accurate home inventory can help provide insurers with information needed to settle claims.

Store a protected backup away from the property or in a secure cloud account. Do not publicly share photos that expose keys, alarm panels, access codes, security layouts, identifying documents, or expensive items.

Recurring calendar and open-item list

Keep a simple schedule of manufacturer-required maintenance, professional service, permit or inspection deadlines, warranty expirations, and tasks you plan to monitor. Separate completed work from open priorities.

The monthly maintenance checklist provides a repeatable observation routine, while the seasonal guide helps spread larger tasks across the year.

Store records securely

  • Use clear file names and dates
  • Keep at least one protected backup of important records
  • Restrict access to insurance, account, alarm, and identity information
  • Do not email completed binder pages unless necessary and securely requested
  • Review the record once a year and remove duplicates
A binder is an index, not a vault.

Use it to find the right information quickly, but store original legal documents, passwords, financial records, and sensitive security details using appropriate safeguards.

How long should you keep home maintenance records?

There is no single retention period for every home document. Equipment history, an open warranty claim, property ownership, insurance matters, and tax records serve different purposes.

IRS Publication 530 says records supporting a property's basis generally remain relevant during ownership and afterward for the applicable limitations period. Check current guidance and your circumstances before discarding tax or legal records; do not apply a blanket three-year rule to everything.

Keep useful service history with the equipment record, and confirm claim, coverage, and retention requirements with the relevant provider or qualified adviser. An annual review is a chance to organize records, not an instruction to destroy them.
