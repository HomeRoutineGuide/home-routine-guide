Home emergency kit checklist for new homeowners

Build a useful kit around the people, pets, local hazards, medications, and home systems you actually have—without panic buying or relying on a one-size-fits-all shopping list.

Prepare for your household and local risks.

Ready.gov recommends supplies that can support a household independently for several days, then adapting the kit for children, older adults, disabilities, medications, pets, climate, and local hazards. Follow emergency alerts and instructions from local authorities during an actual event.

September is National Preparedness Month, making this a useful time to assemble a first kit or review one you already have. A kit is only one part of preparedness: you also need an emergency communication plan, verified contacts, alerts, evacuation information, and a safe way to access important records.

The home emergency kit starter checklist

Use this as a planning list, not a promise that every emergency can be handled at home. Quantities, storage, replacement schedules, and evacuation needs depend on your household and local guidance.

1. Plan water and food for several days

Ready.gov advises preparing to be self-sufficient for several days. Water and food needs vary with the number and ages of household members, health conditions, pets, climate, and the type of emergency.

  • Use sealed bottled water or clean, food-grade storage containers as directed by public-health guidance.
  • Choose shelf-stable foods the household can safely eat, including options that do not require refrigeration or extensive cooking.
  • Include a manual can opener when canned food is part of the plan.
  • Record expiration or rotation dates instead of assuming stored supplies remain usable indefinitely.
Do not improvise with contaminated water.

CDC says water containing fuel, toxic chemicals, or radioactive material cannot be made safe by boiling or disinfecting. Follow your health department’s instructions and use a different safe source. Never use water from a home heating radiator or boiler for drinking, cooking, or hygiene.

2. Prepare light, power, alerts, and communication

  • Keep flashlights or battery-powered lanterns where they are easy to reach; avoid relying on candles.
  • Include compatible spare batteries, charging cables, and an appropriate backup power bank.
  • Have a battery-powered or hand-crank radio and know which official local alert systems apply.
  • Write down important numbers in case a phone is unavailable, lost, or out of power.
  • Choose an out-of-area contact and household meeting places as part of a separate communication plan.

Test equipment on a recurring schedule and replace damaged, recalled, leaking, or expired items according to manufacturer instructions.

3. Account for health, medicine, first aid, and sanitation

  • Keep an appropriate first-aid kit and learn how to use its contents before an emergency.
  • Plan for prescription medicines, over-the-counter needs, glasses, hearing devices, mobility aids, and other health supplies.
  • Ask a pharmacist or healthcare professional how to plan for medicine refills and temperature-sensitive products; do not change doses or storage on your own.
  • Include soap, hand sanitizer, toilet supplies, menstrual products, diapers, masks, waste bags, and other household sanitation needs.
  • Keep a written list of allergies, medications, dosages, prescribers, pharmacies, and emergency medical contacts where appropriate.

Call emergency services for immediate medical danger. A household kit is not a substitute for professional care or an evacuation order.

4. Protect essential contacts, documents, and payment access

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends organizing important records before a disaster and keeping protected copies in more than one safe location.

  • Create a concise list of household contacts, utilities, insurance providers, medical contacts, and local emergency resources.
  • Identify the records you might need for identification, insurance, property, medical care, or disaster assistance.
  • Store physical copies in an appropriate fire- and water-resistant location.
  • Protect digital copies with strong access controls and keep a separate backup.
  • Keep an appropriate amount of cash because electronic payment systems may be unavailable.

Do not place passwords, full account numbers, Social Security numbers, or unprotected identity documents on a sheet left in public view. Use the Emergency Information Checklist to decide what belongs on an accessible contact sheet and what requires secure storage.

5. Learn how an outage affects your specific home

A new homeowner’s emergency plan should include the systems that may stop, alarm, lock, drain, freeze, overheat, or lose monitoring when power or communications fail.

  • Learn the manual release procedure for an electric garage door from the manufacturer instructions.
  • Understand what happens to well pumps, sump pumps, sewage equipment, security systems, elevators, smart locks, and internet-dependent devices.
  • Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms and keep them active during an outage.
  • Keep refrigerator and freezer thermometers. FDA says an unopened refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about four hours; follow current food-safety guidance rather than relying on appearance or odor.
  • Use the main water shutoff guide to document the verified control, but never force an unfamiliar or damaged valve.
Portable generators create deadly carbon-monoxide risk.

CPSC says portable generators must be used outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the home, with exhaust directed away from the home and other buildings. Never use a generator inside a home, garage, basement, shed, crawlspace, porch, or carport, and never use a gas stove or oven to heat the home. Follow the generator manufacturer and current local emergency guidance.

6. Add the needs that generic lists miss

  • Children: feeding, comfort, hygiene, identification, and age-appropriate communication needs.
  • Older adults and disabilities: mobility, communication, backup power, caregiver contacts, transportation, and accessible evacuation needs.
  • Pets and service animals: food, water, medication, carriers, leashes, sanitation, records, identification, and pet-friendly evacuation options.
  • Climate: safe seasonal clothing, blankets, sun protection, rain gear, or other locally appropriate items.
  • Local hazards: supplies and plans recommended by local emergency management for storms, flooding, wildfire, earthquake, extreme heat, winter weather, or other risks.

Make sure every household member knows where appropriate supplies are kept and which items adults should operate.

7. Store, label, and review the kit

  • Choose a dry, accessible place that is appropriate for the products and reachable during likely emergencies.
  • Consider a smaller portable evacuation kit in addition to home supplies.
  • Label containers clearly and keep hazardous items away from children, pets, food, and incompatible materials.
  • Set a recurring review date for food, water, medicine, batteries, clothing sizes, documents, contacts, and equipment condition.
  • Update the kit after a move, household change, new medical need, pet adoption, equipment replacement, or change in local risk.
