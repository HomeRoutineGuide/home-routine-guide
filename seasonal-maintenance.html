Four-season routine

A manageable year of home maintenance

Use each season as a reminder to observe the property, handle a few recurring tasks, and plan professional service before peak demand.

Maintenance is not one universal calendar.

A cold-climate house with a boiler and basement has different needs from a hot-climate condo with a heat pump. Use this as a structure, then adapt it to your property.

A short monthly routine

The best maintenance system is one you can repeat. A five-to-fifteen-minute monthly check can help you notice changes without creating a second job.

  • Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms according to manufacturer guidance and local requirements.
  • Look under sinks and around visible plumbing for drips, stains, swelling, or unusual moisture.
  • Check the HVAC filter and follow the equipment manufacturer’s replacement instructions.
  • Glance at mechanical areas for unusual sounds, odors, corrosion, or water.
  • Confirm important drains, sump areas, and exterior discharge points are not visibly blocked.
  • Record anything that changed and decide whether to monitor, schedule service, or act promptly.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends monthly testing of smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms. ENERGY STAR recommends checking central HVAC filters monthly during heavy-use periods and replacing them when dirty.

Spring: inspect and recover

Spring is a useful time to look for winter-related changes and prepare cooling, drainage, and exterior areas for wetter and warmer weather.

Exterior and drainage

  • Walk the property from the ground and compare it with earlier photos.
  • Look for displaced materials, damaged trim, loose railings, new cracks, staining, or erosion.
  • Observe whether downspouts and surface grading direct water away from the structure.
  • Check accessible gutters and drainage paths from a safe location; hire qualified help when ladder or roof access is required.
  • Look for vegetation touching siding, roofing, vents, or outdoor equipment.

Cooling and ventilation

  • Arrange pre-season cooling-system service when appropriate for your equipment.
  • Confirm outdoor equipment has the clearance required by the manufacturer.
  • Check that supply and return openings are not covered by furniture or storage.
  • Review bathroom, kitchen, and dryer exhaust outlets for visible blockage or damage.

Water and outdoor areas

  • Inspect outdoor faucets, irrigation, hoses, and exposed plumbing after freezing weather.
  • Check decks, stairs, balconies, porches, and handrails for movement or deterioration.
  • Review window and door screens, weather seals, and visible caulk joints.

Summer: monitor heat, water, and wear

Summer maintenance is mostly about keeping systems efficient, watching moisture, and handling exterior work under safe conditions.

Cooling system

  • Continue checking the filter according to system instructions.
  • Notice changes in runtime, comfort, drainage, noise, or odor and arrange service rather than guessing at the cause.
  • Keep outdoor equipment free of loose debris while respecting manufacturer clearance and electrical safety.

Moisture and storms

  • Check lower levels, crawlspaces, attics, and mechanical areas that are safely accessible for signs of moisture or condensation.
  • After heavy rain, observe drainage, sump operation, roof leaks, and water entry points.
  • Document storm damage promptly for repair and insurance conversations.

Exterior safety

  • Inspect walking surfaces, exterior lighting, steps, gates, and railings.
  • Keep grills, fire features, and fuel-burning equipment positioned and used according to manufacturer and local fire-safety guidance.
  • Review pool, spa, play-equipment, and fence safety where applicable.

Fall: prepare before cold weather

Fall is the main planning season for heating, weather protection, leaves, drainage, and cold-weather emergencies.

If this is your first fall in the property, use the focused fall home maintenance checklist for new homeowners to work through the priorities in order.

Heating and combustion safety

  • Arrange pre-season heating service appropriate to the system and service history.
  • Have chimneys, vents, fireplaces, and fuel-burning appliances evaluated or serviced as required.
  • Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms and confirm suitable placement.
  • Never use an oven, grill, or generator to heat an indoor space.

Exterior and weather protection

  • Review gutters and drainage after major leaf drop.
  • Look for damaged weatherstripping, missing sealant, and openings around exterior penetrations.
  • Follow local and manufacturer guidance for winterizing outdoor faucets, irrigation, pools, and seasonal equipment.
  • Store hoses and outdoor items so they do not create trip or storm hazards.

Emergency readiness

  • Confirm flashlights, batteries, charging options, medications, and emergency contacts are current.
  • Know how heating, water, pumps, and essential equipment behave during an outage.
  • Review generator safety before an emergency; CPSC says portable generators belong outside, at least 20 feet from the home, with exhaust directed away.

Winter: watch safety and cold-weather stress

Winter routines emphasize alarms, vents, moisture, freezing, storms, and safe heating practices.

Alarms and fuel-burning equipment

  • Continue monthly smoke and carbon-monoxide alarm tests.
  • Keep snow, ice, and debris away from exterior vents for fuel-burning appliances.
  • Never ignore an alarm. Leave the home and contact emergency services when instructed or when carbon monoxide is suspected.

Water and freezing conditions

  • Watch for unusual condensation, low water flow, cold areas around plumbing, or new moisture.
  • Follow utility, local, and professional guidance for freeze prevention appropriate to your plumbing and climate.
  • Know the main water shutoff location before a pipe failure occurs.

Storm observation

  • Inspect from the ground after severe weather and document visible changes.
  • Use qualified help for roof, ladder, tree, electrical, or structural hazards.
  • Keep exits, walkways, fire hydrants where required, and essential equipment safely accessible.

Turn the list into your own year

Build a personal calendar from four inputs:

  1. Home profile: structure type, age, climate, utilities, equipment, and special features.
  2. Manufacturer schedule: required inspection, cleaning, replacement, and warranty tasks.
  3. Professional history: last service date, condition, open recommendations, and permits.
  4. Your observations: recurring moisture, drainage, comfort, pest, or wear patterns.

Do not overload one weekend. Spread tasks across the season, bundle related professional visits, and keep a record of completion, cost, and findings.
