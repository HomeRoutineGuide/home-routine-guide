Your inspection report is a starting record—not a complete diagnosis or warranty.

Preserve the original report, confirm what was completed before closing, and follow the inspector’s recommendations for qualified evaluation. Call emergency services or the appropriate utility for an immediate threat.

Before you sort the findings

Save the original report and related photographs in secure storage. Collect any seller repair receipts, paid invoices, permits, warranties, or reinspection notes so you can separate completed work from open questions.

Read the report’s summary and the full entry for each finding. A short summary may omit context, limitations, photographs, or a recommendation for specialist evaluation. Record the report page or section so you can return to the original language later.

Do not upload a private inspection report to use this tool.

Choose follow-up categories carefully

Use the inspector’s language or a qualified professional’s recommendation to place each item into a working category:

  • Professional follow-up: the report recommends specialist evaluation, repair, or immediate attention.
  • Schedule promptly: an active condition or important functional problem needs a defined next step.
  • Plan and budget: work is known but timing, scope, or cost still needs to be developed.
  • Monitor and maintain: preserve the observation, follow stated maintenance guidance, and note meaningful changes.
  • Confirm completed: the seller or another party agreed to address the item before closing; retain evidence and ask the appropriate professional about verification.

Do not downgrade an item because it looks small in a photograph or upgrade it based on an online cost estimate. The report author and relevant qualified professionals can explain the finding, limitations, and next evaluation.

Turn a finding into a useful repair scope

A contractor needs more than “fix the plumbing” or “repair the roof.” Keep the original finding available and ask each bidder to identify the proposed scope, materials or equipment, exclusions, permit responsibility, schedule, payment terms, cleanup, and warranties.

When the report recommends further evaluation, obtain that evaluation before comparing repair prices. Different contractors may otherwise quote different problems and scopes.

Attach real estimates to the plan

Replace rough allowances with written estimates as the scope becomes clearer. Keep routine maintenance, known projects, and an emergency reserve separate so one large item does not hide the rest of the plan.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau advises homebuyers to account for maintenance and repairs in the full cost of ownership. No universal percentage can predict the needs of a particular property.

Close the loop when work is completed

For completed work, retain the final written scope, invoice, payment record, permit or inspection record where applicable, warranty information, contractor contact details, completion date, and useful before-and-after photographs. Record what changed and any follow-up date.
