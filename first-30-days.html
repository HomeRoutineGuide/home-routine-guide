New homeowner guide

Your first 30 days in a new home

A prioritized plan for learning the house, handling safety basics, organizing important records, and building a maintenance routine without trying to do everything at once.

You do not need to “finish” your house in 30 days.

The goal is to reduce avoidable risk, understand the major systems, and create a reliable place for information. Cosmetic projects can wait.

Day one: access, safety, and shutoffs

Secure exterior access

Rekey or replace exterior locks, update garage and keypad codes, and remove access for prior owners, contractors, or temporary users. Include detached garages, sheds, gates, and smart-home accounts where applicable.

Locate the main controls

Find the main water shutoff, electrical service panel, and any fuel shutoff that applies to the home. Do not experiment with unfamiliar gas or oil controls. Ask the utility, equipment provider, home inspector, or a qualified professional to show you the correct procedure.

Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms

Confirm the home has working alarms in the locations required by local law and manufacturer instructions. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends testing smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms monthly and placing smoke alarms on every level, outside sleeping areas, and inside bedrooms. It recommends carbon-monoxide alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas.

Identify emergency exits and supplies

Make sure doors and windows used for emergency escape open properly. Locate fire extinguishers, flashlights, first-aid supplies, and any building-specific emergency equipment. Read the instructions before an emergency occurs.

First week: learn what you own

Photograph equipment labels

Take clear photos of the model and serial labels for the furnace, boiler, heat pump, air-conditioning equipment, water heater, electrical equipment, pumps, and major appliances. Record installation dates when known.

Collect warranties and service history

Gather closing documents, inspection reports, permits, manuals, receipts, warranty records, service stickers, and contractor information. Keep originals where appropriate and create a simple digital backup.

Inspect visible plumbing areas

Look under sinks and around toilets, tubs, showers, washing machines, dishwashers, water heaters, and exposed piping. You are looking for active drips, staining, swelling, corrosion, musty odors, or new moisture—not performing invasive inspection.

Check the HVAC filter

Find the correct filter location and size, then follow the equipment manufacturer’s instructions. ENERGY STAR recommends checking central HVAC filters monthly during heavy-use periods and replacing them when dirty; some systems have different requirements.

Turn the inspection report into a working plan

Use the report’s language and recommended follow-up rather than assigning urgency from a photo or online estimate. Confirm which repairs were completed before closing, keep evidence of the work, and use the free inspection report action planner to organize open findings, target dates, estimates, and status.

Weeks two through four: establish the routine

Walk the exterior slowly

Use daylight and look from the ground. Notice drainage paths, gutters, downspouts, grading, roof edges, siding, trim, steps, railings, decks, exterior outlets, penetrations, and vegetation touching the structure. Photograph anything you plan to monitor. Do not climb onto a roof unless you are trained and equipped to do so safely.

Learn the normal sounds and cycles

Pay attention to how the heating, cooling, plumbing, pumps, and appliances normally start, run, and stop. A basic sense of “normal” can help you recognize changes later without diagnosing the cause yourself.

Create a monthly five-minute check

Choose one recurring date to test alarms, look under sinks, glance at equipment areas, inspect the HVAC filter, and check for unusual moisture or odors. Keep the routine short enough that you will actually repeat it.

Schedule appropriate professional service

If service history is unknown, consider asking qualified professionals about the condition and maintenance needs of systems such as heating, cooling, chimneys, septic systems, wells, pools, generators, elevators, or specialized fire-safety equipment. ENERGY STAR recommends annual pre-season HVAC checkups by a contractor.

Build a simple home record

A useful home record does not need expensive software. Start with:

  • Property address and emergency contacts
  • Utility providers and account information
  • Shutoff and panel locations
  • Equipment model, serial, age, and warranty data
  • Contractor and service history
  • Receipts, permits, and before-and-after photos
  • Known recurring tasks and service dates
  • Open inspection items and repair priorities

A good record helps you communicate with contractors, track repeated problems, support warranty claims, and prepare for a future sale.

Start a maintenance budget

There is no universal percentage that accurately predicts maintenance for every property. Costs vary with climate, age, size, construction, condition, systems, labor prices, insurance, and owner choices. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau advises buyers to budget for maintenance, repairs, utilities, and emergency savings in addition to the mortgage payment and other ownership costs.

Start with a realistic monthly amount you can sustain. Then improve the estimate by listing known near-term work, aging equipment, insurance deductibles, and recurring professional services. A modest fund that is reviewed regularly is more useful than a confident but inaccurate rule of thumb.

Avoid the first-month spending trap.

Prioritize safety, active damage, weather protection, and required repairs before decorative projects, furniture upgrades, or convenience purchases.

Situations that deserve professional help

Stop and seek qualified help when a task involves suspected gas leaks, electrical hazards, structural movement, active roof leakage, sewage, major water intrusion, fire or carbon-monoxide alarms, asbestos or lead concerns, mold requiring evaluation, unstable ladders or roofs, or any system you do not understand.

Call emergency services or the appropriate utility immediately when there is an immediate threat. This guide is not a substitute for local codes, manufacturer instructions, an inspection, licensed trade advice, or emergency guidance.
