A home project planner is an organizing page, not a substitute for a written estimate, signed contract, permit, design, inspection, or professional scope. Its job is to keep your goals, questions, decisions, dates, costs, and document references connected from idea through closeout.

What to include in a home project planner

1. The problem and desired result

Write what is happening now, what “finished” should mean, and how you will recognize a successful result. Keep observations separate from an unverified diagnosis.

2. Must-haves, preferences, and exclusions

Separate requirements from optional choices. Also list what is deliberately outside the project so estimates can be compared against the same boundaries.

3. Questions and unknowns

Record access questions, hidden-condition risks, material decisions, permit responsibility, cleanup, protection of occupied areas, and what happens if the scope changes.

4. Estimates and selection notes

The FTC advises homeowners to get multiple written estimates and compare more than the lowest number. A useful estimate identifies the work, materials, completion timing, and price. Keep each original estimate and use the planner to note differences.

5. Budget and payment milestones

Track the approved price, owner-purchased items, documented changes, payments made, and remaining amount. Do not place full bank or payment-card information on a household worksheet.

6. Schedule, decisions, and closeout

Record the expected start and completion dates, selection deadlines, key decisions, change approvals, inspection or permit status when applicable, final invoice, warranty, product details, and photos.

A simple project sequence

  1. Define: document the current condition, desired result, boundaries, and open questions.
  2. Compare: collect written estimates based on the same requested outcome and examine scope differences.
  3. Confirm: verify requirements and put the agreed scope, price, schedule, responsibilities, and changes in writing.
  4. Track: note decisions, approved changes, payments, and unresolved items while the work is active.
  5. Close: collect the final invoice, warranty, manuals, model information, photos, permit or inspection records where applicable, and future maintenance instructions.
Compare scope before price

Two estimates can look similar while covering different preparation, materials, permits, cleanup, protection, disposal, or warranty terms. Use the free contractor quote comparison tool to place those differences side by side.

What the planner should not do

  • Diagnose a structural, electrical, plumbing, environmental, or other technical condition.
  • Replace a written estimate, signed contract, design, permit, inspection, or qualified professional advice.
  • Assume licensing, permit, cancellation, deposit, or contract rules are identical everywhere.
  • Store account passwords, payment credentials, or unnecessary sensitive information.
