Printable maintenance records

What should you track in a home maintenance log?

A useful maintenance log should make the next decision easier. Record what was done, when it happened, who handled it, what it cost, and what needs attention next.

Updated August 2026About 8 minutesHome recordkeeping guide
Keep it simple

One line per meaningful service or repair is usually enough. The goal is a usable history—not perfect documentation.

See the fields to include

A home maintenance log is different from a checklist. A checklist tells you what to look at next. A log records what actually happened so you can see service history, recurring problems, costs, and upcoming needs.

Fields to include in a printable maintenance log

Use a simple table or one-page form with these fields:

  • Date: when the inspection, maintenance, service, or repair happened
  • System or area: HVAC, plumbing, roof, appliance, exterior, electrical, or another category
  • Work performed: a short description of what was checked, replaced, repaired, or serviced
  • Provider: contractor, utility, service company, or homeowner
  • Cost: the amount paid when useful for future planning
  • Warranty or invoice reference: enough information to find the original document later
  • Next action: a follow-up date, replacement target, or observation to revisit

What belongs in the log?

Track meaningful work that you may need to remember later. Examples include:

  • HVAC service and filter changes
  • Plumbing repairs and leak corrections
  • Water-heater service or replacement
  • Roof, gutter, drainage, and exterior repairs
  • Appliance service
  • Smoke or carbon-monoxide alarm replacement
  • Major painting, flooring, or renovation work
  • Pest, chimney, septic, well, or other property-specific service

What should stay somewhere else?

The maintenance log should point to important documents rather than hold every sensitive record. Store signed contracts, insurance documents, financial records, account credentials, and other sensitive information in the secure location appropriate for those records.

Use the log with a monthly checklist

A simple workflow works well:

  1. Use a monthly maintenance checklist to notice what needs attention.
  2. Record meaningful service or repairs in the maintenance log.
  3. Keep equipment, warranty, and contractor information in a broader home maintenance binder.
A log becomes valuable over time

After a year or two, the record can help you answer questions such as when a system was last serviced, which contractor performed the work, whether a problem is recurring, and what costs may repeat.

Free log or complete binder?

A standalone log is enough when you only need service history. A binder is more useful when you also want emergency information, equipment records, warranties, contractor contacts, seasonal planning, and first-month homeowner pages together.
